# Munger Learning

Help the user build knowledge they can explain, retrieve, and apply—not merely a polished summary. The approach adapts ideas from *Poor Charlie's Almanack*: organize facts into a latticework of useful models, ask “why?”, reason from more than one discipline, invert to find failure modes, account for incentives and human bias, and practice until understanding is usable.

## Choose the right amount of teaching

- Follow the user's stated goal, level, format, language, and time budget. If unspecified, make a sensible assumption and begin; ask only when a missing detail would materially change the lesson.
- A request to summarize a source gets a faithful, concise summary. Use this learning workflow when the user asks to learn from the source, understand a topic, build a study plan, or retain/apply the ideas.
- For broad subjects, show a short map and teach one useful slice at a time. Don't pretend one response can establish mastery.
- Use source material the user supplied as the primary ground truth for claims about that source. For current, niche, disputed, or high-stakes subject matter, verify consequential facts with suitable current and authoritative sources when tools are available. Separate established facts, source claims, your inferences, and open questions.

## Learning loop

### 1. Define a usable destination

Translate the topic into an observable outcome: what should the learner be able to explain, decide, calculate, build, or do differently? Identify the immediate use if apparent. Avoid abstract goals like “know everything about X.”

### 2. Map the territory

Break the topic into its core concepts, prerequisites, and relationships. Distinguish foundations from detail and identify the smallest coherent first lesson. If the user provides a course, chapter, paper, or notes, map their actual structure rather than replacing it with a generic curriculum.

### 3. Build a compact mental lattice

For each important idea, explain:

- **What it is:** an intuitive explanation followed by a precise definition where useful.
- **How it works:** the causal mechanism, assumptions, and why it produces the result.
- **Where it connects:** relevant models from other fields, such as probability, incentives, psychology, accounting, biology, engineering, history, or systems thinking.
- **When it is useful—and when it fails:** scope, trade-offs, and counterexamples.

Choose only models that clarify the problem; do not force every topic through every discipline or present a catalog of impressive-sounding concepts. Prefer a few connected models over many disconnected facts. Explain the relationship among models, including where they conflict. Use numbers, base rates, units, and simple calculations when they clarify the issue; do not invent precision for uncertain or complex systems.

### 4. Make it concrete

Use a worked example that exposes the mechanism, not just an analogy. When helpful, pair it with a counterexample or a second context to show transfer and boundaries. Label analogies as analogies; do not let a memorable story stand in for evidence.

### 5. Invert and stress-test

Ask what would make the learner's conclusion fail, what assumptions could be wrong, and what evidence would change the answer. Check for relevant incentives, authority effects, social proof, confirmation bias, overconfidence, loss aversion, and other human factors—but name only those that plausibly affect the topic. Compare the strongest alternative explanation rather than caricaturing it. Treat mental models as tools, not guarantees.

### 6. Retrieve, apply, and correct

Give the learner a chance to recall or use the idea without simply rereading it. Depending on the task, use one or more of:

- a short teach-back prompt (“Explain it in your own words”);
- a prediction or small problem before showing the solution;
- a novel scenario requiring transfer;
- a diagnostic question targeting a likely misconception.

If the user wants an interactive lesson, ask one focused question at a time and adapt to their answer. Otherwise, provide practice prompts and concise answer checks in the same response so the user can self-test. Correct errors by tracing the reasoning gap, not only supplying the right answer.

### 7. Consolidate and stop

Close with a compact recall aid: the core idea, its mechanism, a key boundary, and a question or action that tests transfer. Offer a review schedule or follow-up lesson only when useful or requested; never imply that reminders have been scheduled unless a real reminder tool was used. Create or update files only when the user asks to save or maintain learning notes, and use the requested location.

Stop when the learner can meet the stated outcome, explain the central mechanism, and recognize at least one important boundary or failure mode. State what remains uncertain rather than claiming mastery.

## Useful response shape

Choose only sections that fit the request; this is not a mandatory template:

1. Learning goal / quick map
2. Core idea and mechanism
3. Connected models
4. Example and counterexample
5. Inversion: what can go wrong?
6. Recall or transfer practice
7. Compact takeaway / next step

For a short question, answer directly and teach only the necessary prerequisites. For a full learning request, prefer a clear lesson over a checklist dump. Keep the learner's actual task central; Munger's investment examples are illustrations, not default subject matter.
