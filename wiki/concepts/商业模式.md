# Business Model

## Definition

A business model describes how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. In investment analysis, it is a core variable for assessing whether a company remains worth holding, particularly when the model’s durability is in question.

The source frames a business model primarily as the mechanism through which a company charges customers and extracts money from market participants, making monetization central to how economic value is captured.
## Key Characteristics

- A changed business model may justify selling a stock.
- Business-model durability can be more important than short-term price movements.
- It is evaluated alongside [[Competitive Moat]] and [[Rationality]].
- The concept concerns structural changes in how a company operates and generates value.
- The source does not specify a particular business-model pattern.
- Business models may conceal weak fundamentals or social costs behind profitable narratives, particularly in speculative capitalism and technology investing.
## Applications

- Evaluating whether an investment thesis remains valid.
- Identifying structural reasons to sell a stock.
- Assessing a company’s long-term durability beyond short-term market performance.
- Comparing business-model stability with competitive advantages and management quality.
