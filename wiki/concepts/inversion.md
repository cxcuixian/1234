# Inversion

## Definition

Inversion is a problem-solving method that examines a situation backward: instead of asking only how to achieve success, it asks what would guarantee failure and how those conditions can be avoided. Charles T. Munger attributes the memorable formulation “Invert, always invert” to the mathematician Carl Jacobi. In practice, inversion identifies destructive actions, incentives, environments, and assumptions before they produce failure.

## Key Characteristics

- Reframes problems by analyzing failure conditions before success conditions.
- Emphasizes eliminating obvious errors and risks rather than relying solely on prediction.
- Examines incentives, behavior, assumptions, and environmental conditions that can produce undesirable outcomes.
- Is particularly useful in complex adaptive systems where direct prediction is difficult.
- Supports practical decision-making by converting vague risks into specific conditions to avoid.
- Can be combined with [[concepts/multiple-mental-models|Multiple Mental Models]], [[concepts/margin-of-safety|Margin of Safety]], and [[concepts/checklist|Checklist]] approaches.
- Seeks durable advantages through the prevention of avoidable mistakes.

## Applications

- **Personal conduct:** Ask which habits or decisions would predictably damage health, relationships, judgment, or reputation, then avoid them.
- **Investing:** Identify the conditions that could permanently impair capital, such as excessive leverage, weak incentives, poor business economics, or paying an unjustifiable price.
- **Business strategy:** Analyze how a company could lose its competitive position and use that analysis to protect its [[entities/moat|Moat]].
- **Education:** Determine which learning practices produce superficial understanding or rapid forgetting, then design instruction to prevent those outcomes.
- **Public policy:** Evaluate unintended consequences, institutional incentives, and failure modes before implementing a policy.
- **Complex systems:** Reduce uncertainty by removing obvious paths to disaster when reliable prediction is unavailable.
- **Organizational management:** Examine how compensation, authority, and reporting structures could encourage misconduct or poor decisions.
- **Consumer and brand analysis:** Apply backward reasoning to understand how a durable business such as [[entities/coca-cola|Coca-Cola]] could damage its brand, distribution, or customer loyalty.
