# Parkinson's disease

## Definition

Parkinson's disease is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized principally by degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and disruption of the nigrostriatal pathway. The resulting dysregulation of motor systems, including the extrapyramidal system, produces characteristic features such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor. It is distinct from drug-induced [[Pseudoparkinsonism]], which can produce similar motor symptoms through dopamine D<sub>2</sub> receptor antagonism without representing the same underlying disease.

## Key Characteristics

- Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra.
- Dysfunction of the [[Nigrostriatal pathway]].
- Dysregulation of the [[Extrapyramidal system]].
- Characteristic [[Bradykinesia]], often accompanied by [[Rigidity]] and [[Tremor]].
- A neurodegenerative disease rather than a primary disorder limited to the extrapyramidal system.
- Provides a clinical comparison for distinguishing Parkinson's disease from [[Parkinsonism]] and drug-induced [[Pseudoparkinsonism]].
- Associated with impaired [[Dopaminergic neurotransmission]] and may be treated with [[Dopamine agonists]] or other dopaminergic therapies.

## Applications

Parkinson's disease is used as a clinical and physiological reference point when explaining the relationship between dopaminergic neuron loss, the [[Nigrostriatal pathway]], and motor dysfunction. It helps distinguish the disease from medication-related extrapyramidal symptoms, including [[Dystonia]], [[Akathisia]], and [[Tardive dyskinesia]]. This distinction is relevant when interpreting [[Rating scales for extrapyramidal symptoms]], comparing [[Typical antipsychotic]] and [[Atypical antipsychotic]] medications, and evaluating treatments such as [[Anticholinergic medications]], [[Beta blockers]], and [[Vesicular monoamine transporter 2]] inhibitors. Parkinson's disease also serves as a reference concept in [[Movement disorder]] research and [[Clinical trials]] involving motor symptoms and dopaminergic mechanisms.
