# Focus Investing

## Definition

Focus Investing is [[Charles T. Munger]]’s method of holding a small number of exceptional investments rather than broadly diversifying across many mediocre opportunities. It involves patiently waiting for favorable opportunities, performing extensive analysis, and committing substantial capital when the probability of favorable results is strongly in the investor’s favor.

The method relies on confidence in business quality, durable competitive advantage, intrinsic value, and the investor’s [[Circle of Competence]]. It accepts volatility and extended periods of inactivity in exchange for selectivity, reduced trading costs, and tax-efficient compounding.

## Key Characteristics

- Concentrates capital in a small number of high-conviction investments.
- Requires patience and a willingness to remain inactive until an unusually favorable opportunity appears.
- Emphasizes extensive analysis of business quality, intrinsic value, competitive advantage, and management.
- Depends on a clear [[Margin of Safety]] and a well-defined [[Circle of Competence]].
- Treats investment decisions as rare, consequential opportunities rather than continuous trading decisions.
- Recognizes that a successful investing lifetime may depend on only a handful of major decisions.
- Accepts short-term volatility when the underlying business remains sound.
- Seeks to reduce taxes, commissions, noise, and mistakes associated with excessive trading.
- Benefits from long holding periods and the effects of [[Compound Interest]].
- Uses [[Multiple Mental Models]] to evaluate opportunities and avoid narrow or emotionally driven judgments.
- Differs from conventional portfolio management by prioritizing selectivity and conviction over broad diversification.

## Applications

- Building a concentrated portfolio of businesses with durable competitive advantages and attractive intrinsic values.
- Allocating substantial capital when a rare opportunity falls clearly within the investor’s competence.
- Avoiding investment activity when available opportunities do not offer a sufficient [[Margin of Safety]].
- Evaluating whether concentration is justified by the investor’s knowledge, analytical confidence, and ability to withstand volatility.
- Applying [[Inversion]] and a [[Checklist]] to identify permanent-loss risks before making a large investment.
- Using the [[Lollapalooza Effect]] to recognize situations in which several favorable factors reinforce one another.
- Reducing portfolio turnover to preserve capital that would otherwise be lost to taxes, transaction costs, and avoidable errors.
- Developing a long-term investment process informed by [[Psychology of Human Misjudgment]], [[Incentive-Caused Bias]], and [[Worldly Wisdom]].
