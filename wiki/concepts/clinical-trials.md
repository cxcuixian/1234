# Clinical trials

## Definition

Clinical trials are structured studies that evaluate the effects, benefits, and risks of medical treatments in human participants. In the context of antipsychotic treatment, they are important for assessing both therapeutic effectiveness and adverse effects that may affect treatment adherence.

## Key Characteristics

- Use predefined protocols to evaluate medical interventions in human participants.
- Assess treatment benefits, risks, tolerability, and clinically significant adverse effects.
- May use randomization, control groups, standardized outcome measures, and prespecified endpoints.
- Treatment discontinuation is an important outcome because adverse effects can influence adherence and the interpretation of treatment effectiveness.
- The [[entities/catie-trial|CATIE trial]] is cited as a large example involving 1,460 randomized subjects; 213 discontinued, including 58 discontinuations attributed to [[concepts/extrapyramidal-symptoms|extrapyramidal symptoms]].

## Applications

Clinical trials are used to compare [[concepts/typical-antipsychotic|typical antipsychotic]] and [[concepts/atypical-antipsychotic|atypical antipsychotic]] treatments, evaluate movement-related adverse effects, and determine how tolerability affects treatment continuation. In antipsychotic research, measures such as the [[entities/simpson-angus-scale|Simpson-Angus Scale]], [[entities/barnes-akathisia-rating-scale|Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale]], [[entities/abnormal-involuntary-movement-scale|Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale]], and [[entities/extrapyramidal-symptom-rating-scale|Extrapyramidal Symptom Rating Scale]] can support systematic assessment of [[concepts/dystonia|dystonia]], [[concepts/akathisia|akathisia]], [[concepts/parkinsonism|parkinsonism]], and [[concepts/tardive-dyskinesia|tardive dyskinesia]]. These findings help relate movement-related adverse effects to treatment adherence and the interpretation of antipsychotic effectiveness.
