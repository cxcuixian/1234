# 2026-09-10

## Definition

A skeptical investment theory that criticizes speculative markets, fashionable technology themes, and excessive faith in American capitalism. It emphasizes decisive risk reduction, long-term saving, delayed gratification, independence from material dependence, and concentrating on matters within one’s control.

## Key Characteristics

- Skepticism toward speculative investing and rapidly rising technology stocks.
- Recognition that market participants may prioritize fashionable narratives and monetization over fundamental value.
- Warning that retail investors can remain exposed to sharp declines after speculative securities surge and form long upper wicks.
- Advocacy of decisive selling when risk becomes excessive, based on the expectation that future opportunities to repurchase will remain available.
- Analysis of the connections among artificial intelligence, data centers, GPUs, optical modules, liquid cooling, electricity generation, and grid stability.
- Concern that unreliable energy infrastructure may impose substantial costs on institutions and speculative investors in high-technology stocks.
- Emphasis on long-term thinking, delayed gratification, contrarian reasoning, simplicity, and remaining within a circle of competence.
- Definition of wealth as the freedom to choose how to live and what work to pursue, supported by saving and reduced material dependence.

## Applications

- Evaluating speculative technology investments and identifying situations in which market enthusiasm has exceeded underlying fundamentals.
- Assessing infrastructure risks associated with artificial-intelligence expansion, including dependence on [[GPU服务器]], [[数据中心]], [[电厂]], electricity, cooling, and grid reliability.
- Establishing personal-finance principles based on saving, delayed gratification, simplicity, and financial independence.
- Deciding when to reduce exposure to high-risk assets rather than remaining committed to a fashionable investment narrative.
- Separating controllable decisions, such as spending and portfolio risk, from uncontrollable market movements.
- Developing a contrarian framework for interpreting technology booms and their effects on institutions and retail investors.
