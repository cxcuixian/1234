# Margin of Safety

## Definition

**Margin of Safety** is the investment discipline of purchasing an asset only when its intrinsic value substantially exceeds its market price, or when the expected return adequately compensates for the risks involved. Associated especially with [[Benjamin Graham]] and applied broadly by [[Charles T. Munger]], the principle aims to reduce the probability of permanent capital loss under conditions of uncertainty, imperfect knowledge, and changing circumstances.

## Key Characteristics

- Compares an investment’s estimated intrinsic value with its purchase price.
- Requires compensation for uncertainty, downside risk, and the possibility of analytical error.
- Focuses on avoiding permanent capital loss rather than merely reducing short-term price volatility.
- Is a practical discipline rather than a precise formula.
- Encourages skepticism toward questionable counterparties, excessive leverage, inflation, interest-rate changes, hidden exposures, and reputational threats.
- Depends on analytical rigor, independence of thought, and a well-defined [[Circle of Competence]].
- Supports concentrated allocation when the investor has both strong understanding and a sufficiently favorable risk-reward relationship.
- Complements [[Risk]], [[Focus Investing]], [[Multiple Mental Models]], and [[Inversion]].
- Helps investors cope with [[Psychology of Human Misjudgment]], [[Incentive-Caused Bias]], and other sources of human error.
- Can be applied beyond securities analysis to business decisions, legal judgments, counterparties, and personal conduct.

## Applications

- Buying shares only when the market price is meaningfully below a conservative estimate of [[Intrinsic Value]].
- Assessing the durability of a company’s competitive position, cash flows, balance sheet, and management before making a concentrated investment.
- Rejecting opportunities whose returns depend on optimistic assumptions, high leverage, favorable interest rates, or fragile financing.
- Evaluating risks that are difficult to quantify, including reputational damage, agency problems, hidden liabilities, and unreliable counterparties.
- Applying independent judgment instead of following [[Mr. Market]], social consensus, or authority.
- Using checklists and [[Worldly Wisdom]] to identify overlooked risks and prevent avoidable errors.
- Maintaining sufficient financial and operational resilience to withstand adverse outcomes.
- Selecting businesses such as [[Coca-Cola]], [[See's Candies]], [[Costco]], [[Teledyne]], or other enterprises whose quality and economics can provide protection against uncertainty when purchased at a sensible price.
- Applying the discipline in legal and organizational contexts associated with [[Munger Tolles & Olson]], [[Berkshire Hathaway]], and [[Wesco Financial]].
