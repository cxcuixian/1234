# Dopamine agonists

## Definition

Dopamine agonists are medications that increase dopaminergic signaling by activating dopamine receptors. In the management of antipsychotic-induced extrapyramidal symptoms, they are generally avoided because enhancing dopamine activity may worsen psychotic symptoms in people taking neuroleptic drugs.

## Key Characteristics

- Activate one or more dopamine receptor subtypes and increase dopaminergic signaling.
- May theoretically improve motor dysfunction associated with reduced dopaminergic activity.
- Are generally avoided for antipsychotic-induced [[Pseudoparkinsonism]] because they can counteract dopamine D2 receptor antagonism and aggravate psychosis.
- Are rarely used for antipsychotic-induced pseudoparkinsonism.
- Their clinical use must be distinguished from treatments such as [[Anticholinergic medications]], [[Beta blockers]], or vesicular monoamine transporter 2 inhibitors, which target different extrapyramidal symptoms.

## Applications

Dopamine agonists have potential relevance to dopamine-related [[Movement disorder|movement disorders]] and motor dysfunction. However, in patients receiving neuroleptic drugs such as [[Haloperidol]], [[Fluphenazine]], or [[Metoclopramide]], they are generally not selected for antipsychotic-induced [[Extrapyramidal symptoms]]. Increasing dopaminergic signaling may worsen psychotic symptoms, limiting their use in [[Pseudoparkinsonism]] and other medication-induced motor syndromes.
