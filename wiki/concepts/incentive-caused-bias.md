# Incentive-Caused Bias

## Definition

Incentive-Caused Bias is the tendency for rewards, punishments, and compensation structures to alter cognition and behavior in self-serving directions. Incentives can lead otherwise decent people to rationalize conduct that harms customers, owners, institutions, or society. The concept is central to [[entities/charles-t-munger|Charles T. Munger]]’s analysis of business ethics, institutional design, and psychological misjudgment.

## Key Characteristics

- Incentives influence not only behavior but also perception, judgment, and interpretation.
- People commonly rationalize actions that serve their financial, professional, or social interests.
- Poorly designed compensation systems can reward short-term results while concealing long-term costs.
- Institutions may produce unethical outcomes without requiring individually malicious actors.
- Incentive effects can combine with other biases to create large, self-reinforcing errors.
- Reliable accounting and transparent measurement are necessary for detecting distorted performance.
- Effective safeguards include aligning rewards with desired outcomes and making cheating difficult.
- Advice that is especially beneficial to the adviser should be treated with skepticism.
- Examples include Federal Express, Xerox, defense procurement, Westinghouse lending, accounting practices, and professional advice.
- The tendency is relevant to business ethics, corporate governance, regulation, sales, finance, and professional services.

## Applications

- **Compensation design:** Tie rewards to durable value creation rather than easily manipulated short-term metrics.
- **Corporate governance:** Examine whether executives, employees, auditors, and advisers benefit from decisions they recommend.
- **Accounting and performance measurement:** Use reliable accounting systems and inspect whether reported results correspond to economic reality.
- **Procurement and contracting:** Design controls that discourage cost inflation, unnecessary complexity, and supplier-driven decisions.
- **Risk management:** Consider how incentives may encourage excessive leverage, hidden risks, or the concealment of losses.
- **Professional advice:** Assess whether advisers are recommending actions because they are suitable for the client or profitable for themselves.
- **Institutional design:** Make undesirable conduct difficult, visible, and costly while rewarding the behavior the institution actually wants.
- **Personal decision-making:** Invert the analysis by asking who benefits from a belief, recommendation, or proposed action.
