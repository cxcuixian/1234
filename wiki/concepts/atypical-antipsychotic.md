# Atypical antipsychotic

## Definition

An atypical antipsychotic is an antipsychotic medication generally described as producing lower rates of extrapyramidal symptoms than typical antipsychotics. This difference is attributed to relatively lower dopamine D2 receptor affinity or relatively higher serotonin 5-HT2A receptor affinity, although adverse-effect profiles vary among individual medications.

## Key Characteristics

- Also known as a second-generation antipsychotic.
- Generally associated with lower rates of [[Extrapyramidal symptoms]] than [[Typical antipsychotic|typical antipsychotics]].
- The reduced risk of extrapyramidal symptoms has been attributed to lower [[dopamine D2 receptors|D2 receptor]] affinity or higher [[Serotonin 5-HT2A receptor affinity]].
- Examples include [[Aripiprazole]], [[Ziprasidone]], [[Quetiapine]], [[Olanzapine]], [[Risperidone]], and [[Clozapine]].
- Lower extrapyramidal symptom risk is not uniform across the class; [[Aripiprazole]] and [[Lurasidone]] may be relatively more likely to cause [[Akathisia]].

## Applications

Atypical antipsychotics may be considered as alternatives to typical antipsychotics when [[Extrapyramidal symptoms]] occur during antipsychotic treatment. Selection among agents requires attention to each medication’s adverse-effect profile, including the possibility of [[Akathisia]] with some second-generation agents.
